▪Overview: Selecting the Appropriate Medicolegal Examination

Type of ExaminationWhen It Is Indicated
Physical/clinical examinationLiving person alleging injury/violence; documents wounds for classification and evidence
Autopsy/necropsyDeath of unknown, sudden, violent, or suspicious cause; all medicolegal deaths
ExhumationBody already buried; a new medicolegal question arises requiring disinterment
Forensic toxicologySuspected poisoning, drug intoxication, or alcohol-related death/offense
Forensic serology (incl. DNA)Identification of blood/semen/saliva stains; linking a suspect to a scene or victim
Psychiatric/mental examinationQuestion of sanity, competency to stand trial, or testamentary/contractual capacity
Examination of living victim of sexual crimeAlleged rape/acts of lasciviousness; genital and extragenital findings, specimen collection
  • Must know: The physician's role is to gather objective medical evidence answering the specific legal question raised by the case (Is the person alive/dead? Was there penetration? Was there poison?) — not to declare guilt or innocence.
  • ETHChoosing the correct examination means matching the medicolegal question to the correct procedure; a mismatched exam wastes the one chance to capture perishable evidence.
  • FORA single case may require more than one type of examination — e.g., a fatal rape case needs both an autopsy for injuries/cause of death and forensic serology for seminal evidence.
Board trap: "necropsy" vs "autopsy" — in Philippine legal-medicine usage the two terms are used interchangeably for the medicolegal postmortem examination of a human body; do not pick a distractor that claims necropsy is reserved only for animals in this context..

▪Physical/Clinical Examination of the Living (Injury Cases)

StepKey Point
HistoryWho, what, when, where, how of the incident, plus relevant past medical history
ConsentWritten informed consent required before the exam, except in emergencies
Physical examComplete head-to-toe survey; describe each injury by number, location, type, size, and direction
DocumentationBody diagrams and/or photographs; record objective findings verbatim, not conclusions
Medical certificateStates the healing period/duration of incapacity, the basis for classifying the injury under the RPC
  • RPCThe actual healing period/duration of medical attendance or incapacity for work determines whether a physical injury is classified as serious, less serious, or slight under the Revised Penal Code.
  • Must know: Findings should be described objectively (size, shape, location, direction) rather than as diagnostic conclusions about mechanism, unless mechanism determination is squarely within the examiner's expertise.
  • FORA follow-up examination is often needed to confirm the actual healing time when the first medical certificate is issued while the wound is still healing.
Board trap: initial vs final medical certificate — a certificate issued before the wound has fully healed is only provisional; the physical-injury classification must be confirmed by a final certificate reflecting the actual number of days of incapacity or medical attendance..

▪Examination of the Living Victim of a Sexual Crime

ComponentKey Point
TimingIdeally within 72 hours, the window with the best yield for spermatozoa/DNA recovery
ConsentWritten consent of the victim, or of the parent/guardian if a minor, before the exam
Genital examinationHymenal lacerations/tears described by clock-face position; degree of distensibility noted
Extragenital examinationBruises, bite marks, defense wounds elsewhere on the body
Specimen collectionVaginal/perianal/oral swabs for spermatozoa and seminal fluid; clothing preserved and sealed
  • SLRA 8505 (Rape Victim Assistance and Protection Act) mandates rape crisis centers to provide free medical, legal, and psychological assistance, including the medicolegal examination.
  • Must know: Absence of hymenal laceration does NOT rule out rape — penetration can occur without laceration in an elastic/distensible hymen or a non-virgin state; the essential element is penetration (even slight) without consent, not visible genital injury.
  • FORMotile spermatozoa suggest intercourse within a few hours; non-motile but intact spermatozoa may still be recovered from the vagina up to 5–7 days after intercourse.
Board trap: negative genital finding vs "no rape occurred" — a normal or unremarkable genital exam is common and does not negate the crime; the corpus delicti of rape rests on proof of penetration plus lack of consent/force, not on visible injury..
  • Medical certificate = the examining physician's objective clinical findings and professional opinion; Sworn statement/affidavit = the victim's own narration of the incident given under oath.

▪Autopsy / Necropsy

TypeDescription
Medicolegal (forensic) autopsyDone by authority of law for violent, sudden, suspicious, or unattended deaths; no family consent needed
Clinical (pathological) autopsyDone to confirm a diagnosis or for academic/scientific purposes; requires consent of next of kin
Complete autopsyAll three body cavities (head, chest, abdomen) are opened and examined
Partial/selective autopsyOnly the region/organ system relevant to the question is opened; used only when legally sufficient
  • RPCFORThe autopsy's key outputs are the cause of death (the specific disease/injury/toxin responsible), the manner of death (natural, accidental, suicidal, homicidal, or undetermined), and, when relevant, the mechanism and time since death.
  • Must know: A complete autopsy is preferred in medicolegal cases to avoid missing findings that could later be contested in court.
  • FORA medicolegal autopsy does not require family consent because it is performed under legal authority (inquest/prosecutorial/court directive), unlike a clinical autopsy.
Board trap: cause of death vs manner of death — cause of death is the specific medical condition that produced the fatal chain of events (e.g., hemorrhagic shock), while manner of death classifies HOW that cause came about (natural/accidental/suicidal/homicidal/undetermined); do not conflate the two..
  • Complete autopsy = all three body cavities opened; performed when full information is legally required; Partial/selective autopsy = only the relevant cavity/organ opened; acceptable only when it will not compromise the medicolegal conclusion.

▪Exhumation

Court order/authority sought→Disinterment witnessed by officials→Autopsy/further exam performed→Reburial
RequirementRule
Legal basisCourt order (or authority through the prosecutor) authorizing disinterment
Presence requiredRepresentative of the court/NBI/PNP, a health officer, and usually a family representative
PurposeTo perform an autopsy or examination not previously done, or to answer a new medicolegal question after burial
LimitationDecomposition may limit findings; exhumation should proceed promptly once ordered
  • Must know: Exhumation is indicated when a death was not adequately investigated the first time, new evidence emerges (e.g., suspicion of poisoning), or the family/court requests re-examination.
  • FORPostmortem changes such as adipocere formation, mummification, or skeletonization affect what can still be determined on exhumed remains.
  • SLExhumation for evidentiary purposes is generally carried out pursuant to a court order in a pending case, or upon request during preliminary investigation/inquest.
Board trap: consent to burial vs authority to exhume — a family's prior consent to burial is NOT consent to future disinterment; a separate court order/legal authority is always required before an exhumation can be validly performed..

▪Laboratory Examinations: Forensic Toxicology & Serology

ExaminationWhat It Establishes
Toxicology (blood/urine/gastric contents/tissue)Presence and quantification of a poison, drug, or alcohol
Serology (blood typing, DNA)Identity/source of blood or body fluid; useful for paternity and linking a suspect to a scene
Chemical/microscopic exam of stainsConfirms whether a stain is blood, semen, or saliva before further identification testing
  • FORIn suspected poisoning deaths, viscera (stomach and contents, liver, kidney, blood, urine) must be preserved and submitted for toxicologic analysis, ideally collected during autopsy.
  • Must know: A negative toxicology result does not automatically exclude poisoning — it may reflect degradation of the poison, the wrong specimen, or delayed collection.
  • FORDNA analysis (from blood, semen, saliva, or hair) is the most definitive serologic method of identification and can link a suspect to evidence with a very high degree of certainty. SUPPLEMENTARY
Board trap: presumptive vs confirmatory tests — a screening/presumptive test (e.g., a color test for blood) only suggests the presence of a substance and is not sufficient by itself for a legal conclusion; a confirmatory test (e.g., DNA analysis, GC-MS) is required before testifying to identity in court..

▪Psychiatric / Mental Examination

IndicationPurpose
Insanity plea (exempting circumstance)Determine if the accused was insane at the exact time the offense was committed
Competency to stand trialDetermine if the accused can presently understand the proceedings and assist counsel
Testamentary/contractual capacityDetermine soundness of mind to validly execute a will or contract
Civil commitmentDetermine if a person is a danger to self/others, warranting confinement
  • RPCInsanity is an exempting circumstance only if it deprived the accused of intelligence/reason at the exact time the crime was committed — not before or after.
  • Must know: Competency to stand trial concerns the accused's mental state at the time of TRIAL, not at the time of the offense; a person may be competent to stand trial yet have been insane during the crime, or vice versa.
  • ETHThe psychiatric examiner must be a neutral, qualified expert whose conclusions are evidence-based, not merely the examinee's self-report.
Board trap: insanity at the time of the crime vs incompetency at the time of trial — these are assessed at different points in time and carry different legal consequences (exemption from criminal liability vs. suspension of the proceedings); board questions often swap the timing to test whether examinees notice..
  • Insanity (as an exempting circumstance) = mental state assessed AT THE TIME OF THE OFFENSE; Incompetency to stand trial = mental state assessed AT THE TIME OF TRIAL/proceedings.

▪Chain of Custody, Corpus Delicti, Dying Declarations & Expert Testimony

Collection→Marking/labeling→Sealed storage→Documented transfer(s)→Presentation in court
ConceptKey Point
Chain of custodyDocumented, unbroken sequence of custody, control, and transfer of evidence from collection to court
Corpus delictiThe "body of the crime" — proof that the specific crime charged actually occurred, not necessarily a physical corpse
Dying declarationStatement by a person conscious of impending death about the cause/circumstances of their own death; a hearsay exception
Expert witnessA physician who gives opinion evidence based on specialized training to help the court understand medical facts
  • SLChain-of-custody documentation must show who collected the evidence, when, how it was packaged/sealed, and every person who had custody, with no unaccounted gap — a gap invites the evidence to be challenged in court.
  • Must know: Corpus delicti does not mean "a dead body"; it means proof that a crime was in fact committed — in a killing, this is proof of death PLUS proof that the death was caused by a criminal act.
  • SLFor a dying declaration to be admissible: (1) the declarant is conscious of impending death, (2) the statement concerns the cause/circumstances of that death, (3) the declarant would have been competent to testify had they survived, and (4) death actually ensues.
  • ETHThe attending/treating physician who testifies about facts personally observed and treatment personally given is an ordinary/fact witness, while a medico-legal officer or specialist called to give an opinion based on medicolegal examination is an expert witness.
Board trap: attending physician vs medico-legal officer as witness — the ATTENDING physician (who personally treated the patient) is called as an ORDINARY/fact witness to what was personally seen and done, while the MEDICO-LEGAL OFFICER/medical examiner (who examined evidence or remains for legal purposes, not for treatment) is called as an EXPERT witness giving an opinion..
  • Attending physician = treated the patient; testifies as an ordinary/fact witness to personal observations; Medico-legal officer = examines evidence/remains for legal purposes; testifies as an expert witness giving an opinion based on specialized findings.
  • Dying declaration = made by a person conscious of their own impending death, concerning the cause of that death; Res gestae = a spontaneous statement made during or immediately after a startling event, admissible even without consciousness of impending death.

▪Identification of the Living and the Dead — Principles & Methods

MethodBasis / Best Use
Anthropometry (Bertillon system)Fixed adult body measurements; historically used for repeat-offender records, now largely superseded SUPPLEMENTARY
Dactyloscopy (fingerprinting)Gold standard for the living — ridge patterns are unique, permanent from fetal life, and unchanged by injury (regenerate to original pattern)
Forensic odontologyDental records/radiographs comparison; teeth survive fire, decomposition, and skeletonization better than any other tissue
DNA typingMost individualizing method overall; required when soft tissue is destroyed or remains are skeletal/fragmentary
Skull-photo superimposition / facial reconstructionUsed when only a skull and an antemortem photograph are available SUPPLEMENTARY
  • Identification is the process of establishing the individuality of a person (living, dead, or reduced to parts); it is a prerequisite to proving corpus delicti in most medicolegal crimes.
  • FORData used in identification are graded by reliability: primary/individualizing data (fingerprints, DNA, dental records) can single out one person to the exclusion of all others; secondary/corroborative data (visual recognition, clothing, personal effects, scars, tattoos) only narrow the possibilities.
  • FORVisual identification by relatives, though commonly used, is the least reliable method because of emotional bias, poor lighting/viewing conditions, and rapid postmortem facial change.
Board trap: fingerprint identification is exclusionary and individualizing — even identical (monozygotic) twins have different fingerprints — while family visual identification is the weakest evidence and should never be treated as conclusive when a primary method is available.

▪Identification of Skeletal Remains — Race, Sex, Age & Stature

DeterminationMost Useful Skeletal Feature
RaceSkull — cephalic index, nasal aperture shape/width, degree of prognathism, incisor shape
SexPelvis (single most reliable bone) — wider subpubic angle and greater pelvic inlet in females; skull is second-best (males: heavier brow ridges, mastoid processes, squarer jaw)
AgeChildren/young adults: epiphyseal union of long bones and dental eruption; older adults: pubic symphyseal face changes and cranial suture closure (less reliable)
StatureLong bones, especially the femur; length is entered into regression/multiplication formulas to estimate living height
  • FORRace estimates in Philippine practice classify remains into broad ancestral groups (e.g., Mongoloid features predominate in Filipino skeletal material — intermediate nasal aperture, shovel-shaped incisors) rather than fine ethnic distinctions. SUPPLEMENTARY
  • FORThe femur is preferred for stature estimation because it is the longest bone and shows the strongest statistical correlation with total body height.
  • Must know: no single bone is diagnostic by itself for every parameter — race, sex, age, and stature estimates from bone are always expressed as a probable range, not an exact figure.
Board trap: pelvis = best for sex, skull = best for race, pubic symphysis/epiphyseal union = best for age, femur = best for stature — examiners routinely swap these pairings as distractors.

▪Fingerprint, Dental & DNA Identification — Special Notes

  • FORThe three basic fingerprint pattern types are loop, whorl, and arch; Philippine law enforcement classifies and files prints using a Henry-system-based scheme.
  • FORForensic odontology is indispensable in mass-disaster and fire victim identification because enamel is the hardest, most decomposition- and heat-resistant tissue in the body; it is also used for bite-mark comparison in assault cases.
  • FORMitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is inherited only from the mother, exists in hundreds to thousands of copies per cell (versus two copies of nuclear DNA), and therefore survives better in old, degraded, or skeletal samples — useful for comparison against any maternal-line relative.
  • SLNuclear/STR DNA is biparental and highly individualizing; it is the standard method for parentage testing and most forensic casework, and DNA evidence is admissible in Philippine courts under the Supreme Court's Rules on DNA Evidence.
DNA TypeInheritance / Best Use
Mitochondrial DNAMaternal line only; high copy number — best for degraded/skeletal remains
Nuclear (STR) DNABiparental; highly individualizing — best for paternity/maternity and fresh biological samples
Board trap: a badly burned body with intact dentition but no viable soft tissue for fingerprinting is identified through dental records, not fingerprinting — do not default to "fingerprints" as the automatic best answer when the vignette specifies burned or skeletonized remains.

▪Death — Definitions and Immediate (Presumptive) Signs

  • Somatic (systemic) death is the irreversible cessation of circulation, respiration, and brain function as a whole; it must be distinguished from cellular (molecular) death, in which individual tissues die at different rates — brain cells die within minutes of anoxia, while skin, bone, and hair cells may remain viable for hours.
  • FORPresumptive (immediate) signs of death — cessation of pulse/heartbeat and respiration, loss of muscle tone, pallor, and insensibility to stimuli — are unreliable in isolation because conditions such as deep hypothermia, barbiturate overdose, or profound shock can mimic them (apparent death/suspended animation).
  • Must know: definitive proof of death requires postmortem changes (livor, rigor, algor mortis, or putrefaction), not presumptive signs alone.
Board trap: a patient who is pale, pulseless-on-cursory-check, and unresponsive after a massive sedative overdose or cold-water immersion is not necessarily dead — presumptive signs can be falsely positive in suspended animation; only postmortem changes confirm death medicolegally.

▪Early Postmortem Changes — Livor, Rigor, and Algor Mortis

ChangeOnset / CourseMedicolegal Use
Livor mortis (postmortem lividity/hypostasis)Begins ~20 min–3 h; becomes fixed by 8–12 hEstimates time since death; a lividity pattern inconsistent with the body's found position proves the body was moved after fixation
Rigor mortisBegins 2–6 h, complete by ~12 h, resolves by 24–36 h; follows a cranio-caudal ("law of Nysten") order — jaw/face first, then large muscle groupsEstimates time since death (variable with ambient temperature, muscle bulk, and pre-death exertion)
Algor mortis (body cooling)Roughly 1°C/hour for the first several hours, then plateaus; affected by ambient temperature, clothing, and body buildRough time-since-death estimate; least precise of the three when used alone
  • FORLividity color can suggest a cause of death: cherry-red suggests carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning; brownish lividity suggests methemoglobinemia (e.g., nitrite poisoning).
  • FORCadaveric spasm is a distinct, instantaneous stiffening of a single muscle group at the moment of death (no preceding flaccid stage), typically seen in violent or highly emotional deaths; a weapon or object clutched in cadaveric spasm proves it was held at the instant of death, ruling out later planting of evidence.
Board trap: cadaveric spasm is NOT ordinary rigor mortis — it occurs instantly, affects one muscle group (classically a clenched hand), and is medicolegally powerful because it authenticates what the victim was holding at the moment of death.

▪Late Postmortem Changes — Putrefaction, Adipocere, Mummification

Algor mortis→Livor mortis→Rigor mortis→Putrefaction→Adipocere / Mummification / Skeletonization
Late ChangeConditions Favoring ItKey Feature
PutrefactionAny environment; driven by endogenous bacteria (mostly anaerobic, from the gut) and autolytic enzymesFirst external sign: greenish discoloration over the right iliac fossa (overlying the cecum) at ~24–48 h, followed by marbling and bloating
Adipocere (saponification/"grave wax")Moist, warm, anaerobic conditions (wet burial, submersion)Body fat converts to a firm, waxy substance that can preserve body form and even old wound patterns for months to years
MummificationHot, dry, well-ventilated conditions (arid environment)Desiccation arrests putrefaction; skin becomes dark, leathery/parchment-like and can preserve the body almost indefinitely
  • Must know: adipocere needs moisture; mummification needs dryness — the two are mirror-image late changes and are a favorite paired board question.
  • FORBecause adipocere and mummification arrest further decomposition, they can allow identification and even wound-pattern analysis long after death, unlike ordinary putrefaction which destroys soft-tissue detail.
Board trap: do not reverse the conditions — adipocere = wet/anaerobic; mummification = dry/hot/ventilated. A vignette describing a body recovered from a swamp with a soft, waxy, grayish surface is adipocere, not mummification.

▪Cause, Mechanism, and Manner of Death; Sudden Death

TermDefinitionExample
Cause of deathThe disease or injury that initiated the fatal chain of events"Gunshot wound, chest"
Mechanism of deathThe physiologic derangement produced by the cause that actually ends life; often nonspecific and never valid alone on a certificateHemorrhagic shock, cardiac tamponade
Manner of deathThe medicolegal/legal classification of the circumstances of death — Natural, Accident, Suicide, Homicide, or Undetermined (NASH+U)Homicide (in the gunshot example, if inflicted by another)
  • FORSudden death is death occurring rapidly and unexpectedly (classically within 24 hours of symptom onset) in a person who appeared to be in usual health, most often from an underlying cardiovascular cause (e.g., occult coronary artery disease, arrhythmia).
  • Must know: sudden, unexpected, and unexplained deaths are medicolegal cases requiring investigation/autopsy precisely because natural, accidental, and criminal causes must all be ruled out.
  • Manner of death may be revised (e.g., from "undetermined" to "homicide") as new evidence emerges — it is a conclusion, not a fixed fact discovered once and never revisited.
Board trap: writing only a mechanism (e.g., "cardiac arrest" or "respiratory failure") as the "cause of death" on a certificate is incomplete and medicolegally unacceptable — the certificate must state the specific disease or injury that started the fatal sequence.
  • Cause of death = the injury/disease that starts the fatal sequence; Mechanism of death = the physiologic pathway (e.g., shock, sepsis) that ends life; Manner of death = the NASH+U legal classification of the circumstances.

  • FORDefense wounds — cuts or bruises on the palms, forearms, or back of the hands — indicate the victim tried to ward off an attack, pointing to homicide.

  • RPCThe medico-legal certificate for a physical injury case must state the location, number, and nature of wounds, the probable weapon/mechanism, and — most importantly for classification under the RPC — the estimated period of medical attendance or incapacity for work.
Board trap: the healing period/duration of incapacity for labor — not the size of the wound, the weapon used, or the amount of bleeding — legally determines whether a physical injury is serious, less serious, or slight under the RPC.
  • Incised wound = clean, sharp edges, no tissue bridging, length > depth, made by a sharp edge; Lacerated wound = irregular, ragged edges with tissue bridging, made by blunt force.

▪Sexual Crimes I: Legal Framework (RPC and RA 8353)

CrimeKey Elements / Notes
Rape (Art. 266-A, as amended by RA 8353)Carnal knowledge through force, threat, or intimidation; OR victim is deprived of reason/unconscious; OR by fraudulent machination/grave abuse of authority; OR victim is under the statutory age (statutory rape) regardless of consent
Statutory rapeCarnal knowledge of a person below the statutory age of consent — consent is immaterial as a defense
Sexual Assault (Art. 266-A, par. 2)Insertion of the penis into another person's mouth or anal orifice, OR insertion of any instrument/object into the genital or anal orifice of another person; may be committed by or against any person
Acts of Lasciviousness (Art. 336 RPC; RA 7610 if victim is a minor)Lewd/lascivious acts committed under the same force, threat, or incapacity circumstances as rape, but without carnal knowledge or the specific insertion acts of sexual assault
  • Must know: RA 8353 (Anti-Rape Law of 1997) reclassified rape from a Crime Against Chastity to a Crime Against Persons, making it a public crime that can be prosecuted de officio, without need of a complaint filed by the offended party.
  • RPCRape by carnal knowledge (par. 1) can only be committed by a man against a woman; sexual assault (par. 2) can be committed by or against any person, using an instrument, object, or a body part other than the penis for insertion into the genital/anal orifice, or the penis into the mouth/anus.
  • SLRA 11648 (2022) raised the age of sexual consent from 12 to 16 years old — carnal knowledge of, or lascivious conduct with, a person below 16 is statutory rape/statutory acts of lasciviousness regardless of claimed "consent," except for a close-in-age exemption for consensual acts between adolescents near in age.
  • ETHThe examining physician documents objective physical, historical, and laboratory findings; the ultimate legal conclusion that "rape occurred" is a judicial determination made by the court, not a medical diagnosis. SUPPLEMENTARY
  • Must know: Subsequent marriage of the offender and the offended party does NOT extinguish criminal liability for rape under RA 8353 (unlike the old repealed "pardon by marriage" provision).
Board trap: absence of hymenal laceration is not proof rape did not occur, and presence of laceration alone is not proof it did — findings must always be correlated with history, extragenital findings, and other evidence, never read in isolation.

▪Disturbance of Mentality: Insanity, Criminal Responsibility and Competency

▪The Cause-of-Death Sequence: Immediate, Antecedent, Underlying, and Contributory

Line/Part (WHO format)Definition
Immediate cause (Part I, line a)The final disease, injury, or complication that directly led to death.
Antecedent cause (Part I, line b/c)The condition, if any, that gave rise to the immediate cause.
Underlying cause (last completed line of Part I)The disease or injury that initiated the entire chain of morbid events ending in death — this is the cause used for vital statistics coding.
Contributory cause (Part II)A condition that adversely affected the course of the fatal disease but is not part of the direct causal chain (e.g., pre-existing diabetes, hypertension).
Underlying cause→Antecedent cause→Immediate cause→Death
  • Must know: Each line of Part I is written as "due to, or as a consequence of" the condition on the line below it, forming an unbroken causal sequence.
  • SLManner of death is a separate classification (natural, accidental, homicidal, suicidal, or undetermined) describing the circumstances of death, distinct from the disease/injury entered as the cause of death.
  • Must know: For deaths from external causes (injury/poisoning), the certificate typically requires both the cause (the injury/mechanism) and a manner classification, which may be left as "undetermined" or "pending investigation" if the medicolegal work-up is incomplete.
Board trap: cause of death (e.g., "hemorrhagic shock secondary to a stab wound of the chest") is NOT the same entry as manner of death (e.g., "homicide") — the two are recorded separately, and manner is a legal/investigative classification, not merely a medical diagnosis.
  • Underlying cause = the disease/injury that started the fatal chain of events (used for statistics); Contributory cause = a condition that worsened the course of dying but did not start the chain, entered in Part II.

▪Common Errors in Completing the Death Certificate

  • FORListing only a terminal mode of dying — "cardiac arrest," "cardiopulmonary arrest," or "respiratory failure" — as the sole underlying cause; every death ends this way, so the certificate must instead state the disease/injury that produced it.
  • Must know: Leaving the underlying-cause line blank, or writing an incomplete causal sequence that does not connect the immediate cause back to an initiating disease/injury.
  • SLEntering a manner-of-death term (e.g., "homicide," "suicide") on the cause-of-death line instead of the actual disease/injury — the two fields serve different purposes and must not be interchanged.
  • Must know: Incomplete or illegible identifying data (age, sex, civil status, exact place/date of death), which delays civil registration and can invalidate the record.
  • SLCertificate signed by a person without personal knowledge of the case and without legal authority to certify (i.e., neither the attending physician nor the proper health/medico-legal officer).

Board trap: "cardiopulmonary arrest" is a mode of dying common to nearly all deaths and is never an acceptable stand-alone underlying cause of death on the certificate.
  • Cause of death = the specific disease, injury, or complication that produced death; Manner of death = the circumstance classification (natural, accidental, homicidal, suicidal, or undetermined).

  • FORThe standard of care against which breach is measured is typically established through expert testimony from physicians of the same specialty, unless the matter is within the common knowledge of a layman (see res ipsa loquitur).
  • A poor outcome or known complication, by itself, is NOT proof of dereliction — negligence must be affirmatively shown, not presumed from a bad result. SUPPLEMENTARY
Board trap: damage without proof of breach and causation (e.g., a patient who suffers a known complication despite correct management) does NOT constitute malpractice — a bad outcome alone is never enough.

▪Standard of Care, "Captain of the Ship," and Vicarious Liability

DoctrineEffect
Standard of careDegree of skill, diligence, and knowledge ordinarily possessed and exercised by members of the same specialty/profession in good standing under similar circumstances; generally established by expert testimony
Captain of the ship doctrineThe operating surgeon is deemed to have complete control and supervision over everyone assisting inside the operating room during the operation, and may be held vicariously liable for their negligent acts committed during that time, even if they are hospital employees
Borrowed servant ruleA hospital employee (e.g., scrub nurse) temporarily placed under the surgeon's direct control and instruction during surgery is treated as the surgeon's own "servant" for liability purposes during that period
Hospital (employer) vicarious liabilityA hospital may be held liable for the negligence of its own employees (staff nurses, resident staff) under ordinary employer-employee/respondeat superior principles, and — under the doctrine of apparent/ostensible authority and corporate negligence — may in some cases also answer for a negligent consultant it holds out to the public as its own staff
  • Must know: The captain of the ship doctrine applies specifically to the surgeon's control during the operation itself, not to negligent acts of hospital staff occurring before or after that period, which remain outside the surgeon's direct control.
  • ETHA consultant/attending physician in a hospital is traditionally treated as an independent contractor, not a hospital employee, unless the hospital held him out to the public as its own staff or was itself negligent in his selection/supervision. SUPPLEMENTARY
Board trap: "captain of the ship" liability is confined to the operative period under the surgeon's direct control — a nurse's charting error the night after surgery, done outside the surgeon's supervision, is the hospital's/nurse's liability, not automatically the surgeon's.
  • Captain of the ship doctrine = surgeon vicariously liable for OR staff negligence during the operation he controls; respondeat superior = employer (e.g., hospital) liable for its own employee's negligence under an ordinary employment relationship.

▪Res Ipsa Loquitur in Medical Negligence

  • Must know: Res ipsa loquitur ("the thing speaks for itself") allows an inference of negligence WITHOUT direct expert testimony on the standard of care, when three requisites concur.
  • Requisites
  • (1) The injury is of a kind that ordinarily does not occur in the absence of someone's negligence;
  • (2) The instrumentality/thing that caused the injury was under the exclusive control of the physician/hospital staff; and
  • (3) The injury was not due to any voluntary act or contribution of the patient.
  • Classic medicolegal examples
  • FORA surgical sponge, gauze, or instrument left inside the body cavity after closure.
  • Injury to a body part far removed from, and unrelated to, the part being operated on (e.g., a burn on the leg sustained during an abdominal operation).
Board trap: res ipsa loquitur only DISPENSES with the need for expert testimony on breach — it does not dispense with proof of damage or causal connection, and it merely creates a rebuttable presumption of negligence, not automatic/conclusive liability.

▪Doctrine of Informed Consent

Requisite for valid consentExplanation
DisclosurePhysician must explain the diagnosis, the nature and purpose of the proposed procedure, its material risks and benefits, feasible alternatives, and the consequences of refusing treatment
CapacityConsent must come from a patient of legal age and sound mind, or from an authorized surrogate (parent/guardian for minors, next-of-kin hierarchy for incompetent adults) when the patient cannot consent
VoluntarinessConsent must be freely given, without fraud, duress, or undue influence
SpecificityConsent must be specific to the particular procedure to be performed, not a blanket/general authorization
  • Must know: A properly obtained informed consent is a valid defense against a claim of battery or lack of disclosure — it shows the physician fulfilled the duty to inform and that the patient knowingly assumed the disclosed risks.
  • ETHInformed consent embodies the bioethical principle of patient autonomy and is likewise required by the Code of Ethics for the Medical Profession.
  • Exception — emergency/implied consent: treatment may proceed without consent when immediately necessary to preserve life or limb, the patient is incapable of consenting, and no surrogate is immediately available.
  • Absence of any consent at all exposes the physician to a claim of battery (unauthorized touching); consent that is obtained but based on inadequate disclosure exposes the physician instead to a negligence-based claim. SUPPLEMENTARY
Board trap: a valid informed consent excuses the physician only from liability for NOT disclosing information or for the touching itself — it does NOT excuse negligent performance of the consented-to procedure; a properly consented surgery can still be malpractice if executed below the standard of care.

▪Abandonment of Patient

  • Must know: Abandonment is the unilateral severance of the physician-patient relationship by the physician, without reasonable notice, at a time when the patient still requires continuing medical attention.
  • Once a physician-patient relationship (duty) is established, the physician must continue care until: the case is validly terminated by cure/discharge, the patient dismisses the physician, the physician gives reasonable notice and time for the patient to secure another physician, or the physician's services are simply no longer needed.
  • ETHA physician is not ethically obligated to accept every patient who seeks his services (except true emergencies), but once he accepts a case, he cannot arbitrarily withdraw from it.
  • Valid grounds for withdrawal (nonpayment, patient non-cooperation, irreconcilable conflict) do not excuse abandonment unless accompanied by adequate notice and arrangements for continuity of care (e.g., timely referral).
Valid withdrawalAbandonment

Case has been effectively concluded or safely transferred to another providerWithdrawal occurs at a critical point (e.g., the immediate post-operative period)
Board trap: abandonment is both a form of malpractice (a breach of the continuing duty of care once undertaken) AND a Code of Ethics violation — the two liabilities are not mutually exclusive.
  • Valid withdrawal = physician gives reasonable notice and ensures the patient can secure continued care; abandonment = physician unilaterally stops care mid-treatment without notice or referral, while the patient still needs attention.

▪Criminal Negligence (RPC Art. 365) and Civil Damages

ConceptKey point

Reckless imprudenceVoluntary but without malice — doing or failing to do an act from which material damage results, by inexcusable lack of precaution given the actor's occupation, degree of intelligence, and the circumstances

Reckless imprudence carries a higher penalty ↑ than simple imprudence ↓.
  • RPCCriminal negligence still requires proof of the same substantive elements as civil malpractice (duty, breach, damage, causation), but the criminal case demands proof beyond reasonable doubt, while a civil action needs only preponderance of evidence.
  • Civil damages recoverable
  • Must know: Actual/compensatory damages — medical expenses, lost income, cost of further treatment.
  • Moral damages — for mental anguish, physical suffering, wounded feelings.
  • Exemplary damages — awarded only when the negligence is shown to be gross, wanton, or reckless, to serve as a public example/correction.
  • Attorney's fees and litigation costs, when justified. SUPPLEMENTARY
Board trap: an acquittal in a criminal case for reckless imprudence (based on failure to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt) does NOT automatically bar a separate civil action for the same negligent act, since the civil action requires only a lower quantum of proof — unless the court expressly declares that the act/omission from which civil liability might arise did not exist.

▪Checking a Scenario: Malpractice (Legal) vs. Code of Ethics Violation

NatureLegal wrong (civil quasi-delict/breach of contract, or criminal negligence)Breach of professional/moral norms set by the Code of Ethics for the Medical Profession

  • Must know: The two frameworks are related but distinct — a single act may be BOTH malpractice and unethical, unethical WITHOUT being malpractice, or malpractice without triggering a separate ethics provision.
  • ETHExample of unethical but not malpractice: self-laudatory advertising, unfair competition, or fee-splitting with no patient harm — the Code of Ethics is violated, but there is no damage/causal connection to any patient to satisfy the 4 D's.
  • Example of both: performing a procedure without valid consent and executing it negligently — this breaches the standard of care (malpractice) and the Code of Ethics' requirement of respect for patient autonomy.
  • Example of neither: a known, disclosed complication occurring despite properly consented, competently rendered care — no breach of standard of care, and no ethics provision violated.
Board trap: a scenario can be a serious ethics violation (e.g., breach of confidentiality, unauthorized disclosure of patient information) even when the underlying medical treatment itself met the standard of care and caused no compensable "damage" in the malpractice sense.
  • Malpractice = legal liability requiring proof of duty, breach, damage, and direct causal connection, decided by the courts; ethics violation = breach of the Code of Ethics for the Medical Profession, decided administratively by PRBOM/PMA, independent of whether all 4 D's of malpractice are satisfied.

  • Patient autonomy is the ethical foundation of informed consent and of the patient's right to accept or refuse any recommended treatment, even life-saving treatment, once mental competence is established.

▪Physician and Medical Students/Trainees

  • ETHSupervision must be graduated to the trainee's level of competence and experience; the supervising physician bears responsibility for ensuring a trainee does not perform a task beyond his/her demonstrated skill without direct oversight.

Basic Bioethical Principles

▪The Four Classic Principles of Biomedical Ethics

PrincipleCore Meaning
AutonomyRespect for a competent patient's right to self-determination – to accept, refuse, or choose among treatments based on their own values.
BeneficencePositive duty to act for the patient's benefit; to promote welfare and provide net-positive care.
Non-maleficence"Primum non nocere" – first, do no harm; avoid imposing unnecessary risk or injury.
JusticeFair and equitable distribution of benefits, burdens, risks, and access to care among patients/society.
  • Must know: These four principles (Beauchamp & Childress) are prima facie duties – each binds unless it conflicts with an equal or weightier duty in a given case; none is absolute.
  • ETHBeneficence is an active, positive obligation (do good); non-maleficence is a passive, negative obligation (avoid harm) – non-maleficence is generally held to carry stronger prima facie weight because avoiding harm is owed to everyone, while beneficence may be limited by burden to the provider.
  • ETHMost PLE bioethics vignettes are actually conflict-of-principles questions – identify which two principles are in tension before choosing an answer.
  • ETHModern frameworks add related values (fidelity, veracity/truth-telling, confidentiality, dignity) as derivative duties that flow mainly from autonomy and non-maleficence. SUPPLEMENTARY
Board trap: beneficence vs. non-maleficence — non-maleficence means simply refraining from harmful action; beneficence requires an affirmative act that benefits the patient. A question asking "the physician should have done X to prevent harm" is testing beneficence, not non-maleficence, even though harm is the topic.

▪Autonomy vs. Beneficence: Paternalism and Refusal of Treatment

ConceptDescription
PaternalismOverriding a patient's wishes/choices "for their own good," based on the physician's judgment of benefit.
Weak (soft) paternalismOverriding choices only when the patient's decision-making capacity is impaired (e.g., delirium, minority, severe depression) – generally ethically justifiable.
Strong (hard) paternalismOverriding the informed, voluntary choice of a clearly competent patient – generally NOT ethically justifiable in modern practice.
Right to refuse treatmentA competent adult may refuse even life-saving treatment; autonomy prevails over beneficence once capacity and voluntariness are established.
  • Must know: A competent patient's informed refusal must be respected even if the physician disagrees, and even if refusal will result in death, disability, or disfigurement.
  • ETHCapacity (decisional competence) is task-specific and must be assessed for the decision at hand – a patient may lack capacity for complex financial decisions yet retain capacity to refuse a specific treatment.
  • ETHClassic exam scenario: a Jehovah's Witness patient refusing blood transfusion – the correct answer is to respect the refusal, exhaust non-blood alternatives, and document the discussion; forcing transfusion or seeking a court order for a competent adult is the wrong answer on most PLE items.
  • ETHAutonomy is not absolute – it may yield to compelling countervailing duties such as public-health/communicable-disease control (justice/harm to third parties) or genuine incapacity.
Board trap: refusal of treatment by a competent adult tests autonomy, not non-maleficence — do not choose "prevent harm to the patient" as the governing principle when the stem clearly establishes the patient is competent and informed; the correct governing principle is respect for autonomy.
  • Weak paternalism = overriding an incapacitated person's choice, ethically defensible; strong paternalism = overriding a competent person's informed choice, ethically indefensible in ordinary care.

▪Truth-Telling, Disclosure, and Confidentiality

▪End-of-Life Ethics

  • Active euthanasia = a deliberate act directly causing death; allowing natural death (withholding/withdrawing care) = permitting the underlying disease to cause death by not providing or continuing non-beneficial treatment.

▪Research Ethics: Codes, Frameworks, and IRB Review

Code/DocumentKey Contribution
Nuremberg Code (1947)First international research-ethics code; established that voluntary, informed consent of the human subject is absolutely essential, arising from Nazi medical experimentation trials.
Declaration of Helsinki (WMA)Ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects; emphasizes independent ethics-committee (IRB) review, favorable risk-benefit ratio, and the primacy of subject welfare over scientific/societal interest.
Belmont Report (1979)U.S. framework naming three core principles – respect for persons, beneficence, and justice – and their practical applications: informed consent, risk-benefit assessment, and fair/equitable selection of subjects, respectively.
  • Belmont Principle → Practical Application
  • ETHRespect for persons → informed consent (and extra protection for subjects with diminished autonomy, e.g., prisoners, children, the cognitively impaired).
  • ETHBeneficence → systematic assessment of risks and benefits; research must maximize possible benefits and minimize possible harms.
  • ETHJustice → fair procedures and outcomes in selecting who bears research burdens and who receives its benefits (no exploiting vulnerable populations while benefits accrue elsewhere).
  • Must know: Research consent differs from ordinary clinical consent – it must additionally disclose the experimental nature of the intervention, that participation is voluntary, that refusal/withdrawal at any time carries no penalty, and any potential conflicts of interest; therapeutic misconception (a subject believing research is intended primarily for their personal benefit) is a recognized pitfall.
  • SLAn Institutional Review Board / Research Ethics Board (IRB/REB) must review and approve a research protocol before enrollment begins, verifying scientific validity, an acceptable risk-benefit ratio, adequacy of the consent process, and protection of vulnerable subjects; it also conducts ongoing monitoring.
Board trap: which Belmont principle is being tested? — a stem about a poorly worded, incomprehensible consent form tests "respect for persons," not beneficence, even though the topic is a consent document; a stem about enrolling only poor/institutionalized subjects for a study that will benefit wealthier patients tests "justice," not respect for persons.
  • Nuremberg Code = first, narrowly focused on absolute necessity of voluntary consent (post-WWII trials); Declaration of Helsinki = broader, ongoing WMA guidance requiring independent ethics-committee review of research protocols.

▪Distributive Justice and Resource Allocation

Allocation PrincipleBasis for Priority
Utility/likelihood-of-benefitPrioritize those most likely to survive/benefit from limited resources – maximizes lives or life-years saved (typical mass-casualty/disaster triage logic).
Egalitarian (lottery/random)Give all who meet basic eligibility an equal chance, regardless of other factors – avoids invidious discrimination but may not maximize benefit.
Sickest-first (urgency)Prioritize the most critically ill/greatest immediate need, as in routine ICU or emergency-department triage outside mass-casualty settings.
First-come, first-servedAllocate in order of presentation/request – simple and transparent but can disadvantage those with less access.
  • Must know: Distributive justice requires that scarce medical resources (organs, ICU beds, ventilators, vaccines) be allocated by ethically defensible, transparent, and consistently applied criteria – never by ability to pay, social status, or personal characteristics unrelated to medical need/benefit.
  • ETHIn mass-casualty/disaster triage, the utilitarian goal shifts from "doing the most for each individual patient" to "doing the most good for the most patients" with available resources – this is a recognized, ethically accepted departure from ordinary sickest-first practice.
  • FORStandard triage categories (commonly color-coded) separate patients into immediate/emergent, delayed, minimal, and expectant (unlikely to survive even with treatment) groups to guide resource allocation under scarcity.
  • ETHSocial-worth criteria (perceived value of a person to society) are widely rejected as an ethical basis for allocation because they violate justice and invite discrimination. SUPPLEMENTARY
Board trap: mass-casualty triage vs. routine sickest-first triage — in an ordinary emergency room, the most critically ill patient is treated first; in a mass-casualty/disaster scenario with overwhelmed resources, the ethically correct shift is toward salvaging the greatest number of viable lives, which can mean deprioritizing patients unlikely to survive even with maximal intervention (expectant category).
  • Egalitarian/lottery allocation = equal chance for all eligible, ignoring likelihood of benefit; utilitarian/likelihood-of-benefit allocation = priority to those most likely to survive or benefit, maximizing overall good from scarce resources.

⚖️Classic Board Distractor Pairs — All Topics

🔬 Medicolegal Examination
  • Medicolegal (forensic) autopsy = performed by legal authority, no family consent needed; Clinical (pathological) autopsy = performed to confirm a diagnosis, requires next-of-kin consent.
  • Complete autopsy = all three body cavities opened, preferred in medicolegal cases; Partial/selective autopsy = only the relevant region opened, acceptable only if it will not weaken the conclusion.
  • Attending physician as witness = testifies as an ordinary/fact witness to personal treatment; Medico-legal officer as witness = testifies as an expert witness giving an opinion on examined evidence.
  • Presumptive/screening test (e.g., a color test for blood) = suggests a substance's presence only; Confirmatory test (e.g., DNA, GC-MS) = required before testifying to identity in court.
  • Insanity exam = psychiatric evaluation asking whether the accused was insane AT THE TIME OF THE CRIME; Competency exam = the same specialty of evaluation, but asking whether the accused can understand AT THE TIME OF TRIAL — the two exams answer different legal questions even though ordered from the same examiner.
  • Chain of custody = the documented, unbroken transfer of a piece of evidence; Corpus delicti = proof that the crime charged actually happened, not a physical body.
  • Dying declaration = a statement by someone conscious of their own impending death, about its cause; Res gestae = a spontaneous statement made during/right after a startling event, with no consciousness-of-death requirement at all.
⚰️ Nature of Medicolegal Cases
  • Cause of death = the specific disease/injury that starts the fatal chain; Manner of death = the NASH+U classification of the surrounding circumstances — never write one where the other belongs.
  • Adipocere = wet, anaerobic conditions producing a waxy, form-preserving change; Mummification = hot, dry, ventilated conditions producing a leathery, desiccated change — a favorite reversed-condition distractor.
  • Lividity inconsistent with the body's found position = proof the body was moved after fixation; Cadaveric spasm = an instantaneous, single-muscle-group stiffening that instead proves what the victim was holding at the exact moment of death.
  • Incised wound = sharp edge, length greater than depth, no tissue bridging; Lacerated wound = blunt force, ragged margins WITH tissue bridging.
  • Hesitation wounds = shallow, parallel cuts near one fatal wound on a self-reachable area — suicidal pattern; Defense wounds = cuts/bruises on palms and forearms — homicidal pattern showing the victim resisted.
  • Insanity (Art. 12, timed to the offense) = a complete exempting circumstance; Diminished mental capacity (Art. 13) = only a mitigating circumstance, penalty ↓ but liability remains.
  • Statutory rape = age below the statutory threshold makes "consent"/the sweetheart defense legally immaterial; Rape by force, threat, or intimidation = still requires independent proof of force/lack of consent regardless of the victim's age.
  • Cyanide poisoning = cherry-red discoloration PLUS bitter-almond odor and a positive cyanide assay; Carbon monoxide poisoning = the same cherry-red discoloration but confirmed only by an elevated COHb level — cherry-red alone never tells the two apart.
📋 Medicolegal Report
  • History = the account given by the patient/informant, unverified; Findings = objective data the examiner personally observed and measured — writing the former inside the latter is a classic reporting error.
  • Attending physician certifying death = valid only if he/she personally attended the fatal illness; Health officer certifying death = steps in strictly when no physician attended, never merely because the family prefers it.
  • Underlying cause of death = the disease/injury that started the whole fatal chain, used for statistics; Contributory cause = a condition that worsened the course of dying without starting the chain, entered separately in Part II.
  • Cause-of-death entry = the specific disease/injury/complication; Manner-of-death entry = the separate natural/accidental/suicidal/homicidal/undetermined classification — the two fields must never be interchanged on the same certificate.
  • Reporting objective medical findings to the family = the physician's proper role; Declaring a specific person "guilty" or "negligent" to the family = an overreach into a legal/manner-of-death conclusion reserved for investigators and courts.
  • Report stating an opinion/conclusion component (e.g., degree of injury, probable cause) = a proper, expected part of the report; Report substituting a legal conclusion for an objective description (e.g., labeling a wound "assault" instead of describing it) = an improper reporting error.
📜 Licensure & Regulation
  • Passing the licensure exam = an academic/testing milestone only; Certificate of Registration plus the physician's oath = the actual legal authority to practice medicine.
  • Reciprocity = full registration without taking the exam, conditioned on the foreign country's own law extending the same courtesy to Filipino physicians; Special permit = a temporary, case/event-bound authorization only.
  • Administrative liability (PRBOM/PRC, substantial evidence) = affects the license/privilege to practice; Criminal liability (regular courts, proof beyond reasonable doubt) = affects liberty via imprisonment — the very same negligent act can trigger both, independently.
  • Illegal practice of medicine = committed by one never licensed, or by one whose license is suspended/revoked/lapsed; Malpractice = committed by a duly and currently licensed physician who practiced below the standard of care.
  • Non-compliance with CPD units = a ground to withhold RENEWAL, not an automatic revocation of a license already validly held for its current period; Practicing on an expired, unrenewed license = treated exactly like practicing without any license at all.
  • "Insanity" as a criminal-law exempting circumstance (Art. 12, assessed at the time of the accused's crime) = one legal use of the word; "Insanity/mental incapacity" as a ground for suspension or revocation of a physician's own license under RA 2382 = a completely different legal context that happens to reuse the same word.
🏥 Malpractice & Professional Practice Acts
  • Malpractice/negligence = a legal wrong requiring all 4 D's (duty, dereliction, damage, direct causation), decided by the courts; Code of Ethics violation = a breach of an ethical provision decided administratively by PRBOM/PMA, regardless of whether the 4 D's are ever satisfied.
  • Criminal reckless-imprudence charge for a negligent act = needs proof beyond reasonable doubt; Civil malpractice suit for the identical act = needs only a preponderance of evidence — an acquittal in the first does not defeat the second.
  • Captain of the ship doctrine = the surgeon is vicariously liable for OR-team negligence during the operation he directly controls; Respondeat superior = the hospital/employer is liable for its own employee's negligence under an ordinary employment relationship, any time, not just intraoperatively.
  • Valid withdrawal from a case = reasonable notice plus referral while the patient can still secure care; Abandonment = unilateral, notice-less withdrawal while the patient still needs attention — actionable as BOTH a tort and a Code of Ethics violation at once.
  • Informed consent as a malpractice defense = shields the physician only from a battery/non-disclosure claim; Informed consent as the bioethics topic's autonomy principle in action = the very same signed form, viewed instead as the operational expression of patient self-determination — either way, a validly consented procedure can still be malpractice if performed negligently.
  • Res ipsa loquitur = dispenses only with expert testimony on the standard of care/breach; proof of damage and causation is still required, and liability is only a rebuttable presumption, not automatic.
🤝 Physician Relationships
  • Privileged communication = the patient's narrow LEGAL right to block disclosure in a court proceeding, requiring four specific requisites; Confidentiality = the physician's broader ETHICAL duty to protect patient information in every setting, not only in litigation.
  • Curbside/informal consult between physicians = does NOT by itself create a physician-patient relationship with the consulted specialist; Formal referral/second opinion = does create that relationship once the specialist actually examines the patient or assumes management.
  • Genuine, substantiated disclosure of a colleague's actual prior error to protect the current patient = proper and expected; Exaggerated, unsubstantiated put-down of a colleague in front of the patient = unethical disparagement.
  • Midwife managing a NORMAL delivery independently = within her licensed scope; Midwife continuing to manage a delivery after signs of complication or fetal distress = a scope-of-practice violation for failing to refer.
  • Respondeat superior = the hospital answers for its own EMPLOYED physician's negligence; Corporate negligence/apparent authority = the hospital instead answers for a non-employee CONSULTANT's negligence, through bad credentialing or holding him out as its own staff.
  • Industry benefit tied to a prescribing quota (quid pro quo) = unethical regardless of peso value; Modest, unconditional CME sponsorship = generally acceptable — the deciding factor is the string attached, not the size of the gift.
🧭 Basic Bioethical Principles
  • Weak (soft) paternalism = overriding an INCAPACITATED person's choice, generally defensible; Strong (hard) paternalism = overriding a COMPETENT person's informed choice, generally indefensible.
  • Substituted judgment = the surrogate channels the patient's OWN previously known values/wishes; Best-interest standard = the surrogate applies an OBJECTIVE welfare calculus, used only as a fallback when those wishes are unknown.
  • Therapeutic privilege = consent COULD be obtained, but one specific disclosure is withheld because it would itself gravely harm the patient; Emergency exception = there is no time or ability to obtain any consent at all.
  • Withdrawing/withholding life-sustaining treatment (allowing natural death) = the underlying disease remains the cause of death; Active euthanasia = the physician's own deliberate act becomes the direct cause of death — legally and ethically distinct even when both follow the same family request.
  • Nuremberg Code = narrowly established that voluntary informed consent of the research subject is absolutely essential; Belmont Report = a broader framework naming three principles (respect for persons, beneficence, justice) with distinct practical applications.
  • Sickest-first/urgency triage = ordinary emergency-room practice, most critical patient treated first; Utilitarian mass-casualty triage = shifts to maximizing the number of lives saved, which can mean deprioritizing the "expectant" (unlikely-to-survive) category even though they are the sickest.
  • Autonomy governing a competent, informed refusal = the correct governing principle even when the refusal risks death; Beneficence/paternalism used to override that same refusal = the wrong governing principle once capacity and voluntariness are already established in the stem.

🏁Final PLE Mental Checklist — All Topics

🔬 Medicolegal Examination
  • Match the exam ordered to the exact legal question being asked — do not order an autopsy when the real question is testamentary capacity, or a psychiatric exam when the real question is time-since-death.
  • A medicolegal autopsy needs no family consent because it proceeds under legal authority; a clinical autopsy always needs next-of-kin consent — check who is asking and why before assuming either.
  • Never conclude "no rape occurred" from a negative genital or hymenal finding — the crime rests on penetration without consent, not on visible injury.
  • Preserve viscera the moment poisoning is even remotely suspected at autopsy — after burial, only a court-ordered exhumation gets a second chance.
  • A presumptive/screening lab result never supports a courtroom identity conclusion by itself — a confirmatory test must follow before testifying.
  • Sort a physician-witness fast: personally treated = ordinary/fact witness; examined evidence or remains for legal purposes only = expert witness.
⚰️ Nature of Medicolegal Cases
  • Cause of death is the injury/disease; manner of death is the NASH+U classification — they are never interchangeable answers to the same blank on an exam item.
  • If postmortem lividity does not match the body's found position, say so plainly: the body was moved or the scene was staged after lividity had already fixed.
  • Wet plus waxy points to adipocere; hot, dry, and leathery points to mummification — never swap the moisture direction between the two.
  • Hesitation wounds cluster on a self-reachable, usually unclothed area beside one fatal wound and suggest suicide; defense wounds sit on palms and forearms and suggest homicide.
  • Insanity under Art. 12 is timed to the moment of the crime; competency to stand trial is timed to the moment of trial — a person can satisfy one standard without satisfying the other.
  • Below the statutory age of consent, "she/he agreed" and the sweetheart defense are both legally irrelevant — statutory rape needs only proof of age plus the act.
  • Cherry-red discoloration alone never distinguishes carbon monoxide from cyanide poisoning — only a COHb level or a specific cyanide assay settles it.
  • A positive toxicology result proves exposure occurred, never intent — classifying the manner of poisoning still requires the whole scene-and-history picture.
📋 Medicolegal Report
  • Write the patient's or informant's account under History, never under Findings — Findings is reserved for what the examiner personally observed and measured.
  • No physician attended the fatal illness → the city/municipal health officer certifies, not any physician the family happens to prefer afterward.
  • Never enter a mere terminal mode of dying ("cardiac arrest," "cardiopulmonary arrest," "respiratory failure") as the underlying cause of death — name the disease or injury that started the fatal chain instead.
  • Cause of death and manner of death occupy separate fields on the certificate — do not write a manner term ("homicide") where a disease/injury belongs, or vice versa.
  • An unsigned, undated, or illegible medicolegal report carries little evidentiary value in court, no matter how accurate its content.
  • When disclosing a death to the family, report facts, not fault — assigning blame or telling them "you should sue" belongs to investigators and courts, not the bedside.
📜 Licensure & Regulation
  • Passing the boards is not yet a license to practice — registration with the PRC and taking the physician's oath are the two legal steps still missing.
  • A suspended, revoked, or unrenewed-and-lapsed license is treated exactly like never having been licensed at all, for purposes of the illegal-practice offense.
  • Reciprocity registration requires that the FOREIGN country itself extend the same courtesy to Filipino physicians — it is never a one-way grant based on individual merit alone.
  • Administrative revocation needs only substantial evidence plus basic due process (notice and hearing) — it is not a criminal proceeding and never needs proof beyond reasonable doubt.
  • One negligent act by a physician can trigger administrative, civil, and criminal liability all at once, each independently, each judged by its own separate quantum of proof.
  • Missing CPD units blocks a license's future renewal — it does not retroactively undo the validity of services rendered while that license was still current.
🏥 Malpractice & Professional Practice Acts
  • All four D's — duty, dereliction, damage, direct causation — must concur together; a bad outcome or a known complication by itself is never enough for malpractice.
  • The same act can be an ethics violation without being malpractice (no patient damage/causation shown), or malpractice without triggering any specific Code of Ethics clause — screen it against both frameworks separately, not just one.
  • "Captain of the ship" liability covers only the operative period the surgeon actually controls — an error occurring clearly before or after surgery defaults to ordinary hospital/employee liability instead.
  • Res ipsa loquitur only excuses the plaintiff from producing expert testimony on the standard of care — damage and a causal link to the defendant's instrumentality must still be shown.
  • A validly signed informed consent defeats a battery or bare non-disclosure claim, but it never excuses a negligently performed procedure — check execution separately from consent.
  • Reasonable notice plus an actual referral turns a withdrawal from a case into a proper termination; skipping either one turns it into abandonment, which is both a tort and an ethics breach at once.
  • A criminal acquittal resting on failure to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt does not, by itself, wipe out a civil damages award or an administrative revocation arising from the very same act.
🤝 Physician Relationships
  • Confidentiality is the physician's everyday ethical duty in every setting; privileged communication is the patient's narrower legal shield inside a court proceeding — a hallway-gossip problem and a subpoena problem are not the same question.
  • A curbside consult creates no physician-patient relationship with the consulted specialist until that specialist actually examines the patient or assumes responsibility for management.
  • Raising a genuine, substantiated concern about a colleague's care to protect the current patient is proper disclosure; exaggerating or belittling that same concern in front of the patient is unethical disparagement.
  • A midwife refers out the moment a delivery stops being normal — continuing to manage a complicated case alone afterward is a scope violation, not teamwork.
  • Judge an industry benefit by whether it is tied to a prescribing quota, not by its peso value — a small quota-linked gift is ethically worse than a larger, unconditional educational grant.
  • A hospital answers for its employed physicians through respondeat superior, but for a negligently credentialed or ostensibly-held-out consultant through corporate negligence or apparent authority instead — know which theory the facts actually support.
🧭 Basic Bioethical Principles
  • Once capacity and voluntariness are both established in the stem, autonomy outranks beneficence — respect the informed refusal even when it risks death, disability, or disfigurement.
  • Identify weak versus strong paternalism first: overriding incapacity is defensible, overriding a competent adult's informed choice generally is not.
  • Use substituted judgment whenever the stem quotes the patient's own prior words or values, even if a relative is the one speaking; reserve the best-interest standard for when those wishes are genuinely unknown.
  • Therapeutic privilege withholds one specific harmful detail when consent is still otherwise obtainable; the emergency exception applies only when there is no time or ability to obtain consent of any kind.
  • Withdrawing and withholding life-sustaining treatment are ethically equivalent — both let the underlying disease cause death, and neither is euthanasia, where the physician's own act is the direct cause.
  • In mass-casualty triage, the accepted ethical shift is from treating the single sickest patient to saving the greatest number of viable lives — the one recognized exception to ordinary sickest-first practice.
  • A consent form that is technically complete but written so densely that the patient cannot understand it still fails respect for persons — comprehension is the test, not the mere presence of the required text.
